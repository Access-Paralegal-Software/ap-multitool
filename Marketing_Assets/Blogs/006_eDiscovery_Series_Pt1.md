# eDiscovery for Solo & Small Firms (Pt. 1): Information Governance to Identification

When most solo attorneys hear the word "eDiscovery," they think of multi-million dollar class actions and massive server rooms. They think, *"That’s for the Big Law guys. I just have a few hundred emails and some PDFs."*

Here is the reality: If you are practicing law in 2026, you are an eDiscovery attorney. Period. 

Whether you’re handling a car wreck case or a breach of contract suit, the evidence is digital. If you don't have a protocol for handling that digital evidence, you are leaving your client—and your firm—exposed. In this three-part series, I’m going to break down the **EDRM (Electronic Discovery Reference Model)** specifically for the Texas solo.

### Phase 1: Information Governance
This is a fancy way of saying "How do you and your clients keep your stuff?" 
For a small firm, this means having a standardized filing system (like our **Master Case Tree**). If your client’s data is a mess—personal emails mixed with business docs, or files spread across three different cloud drives—the discovery process will be twice as expensive. 

**Pro Tip:** Advise your business clients on a data retention policy *before* litigation starts. It saves everyone a headache later.

### Phase 2: Identification
Once a lawsuit is filed (or even anticipated), the "Clock of Competence" starts ticking. You must identify where the relevant data lives. 
- Is it on a local hard drive?
- Is it in a Gmail account?
- Is it in a Slack or Teams channel?
- Is it on a mobile device?

Identification isn't just about finding the files; it's about understanding the **custodians**. Who created the data? Who had access to it? In Texas practice, missing a key custodian during the identification phase is the #1 cause of "Supplemental Production" panics three weeks before trial.

### The Legal Hold
The moment litigation is reasonably anticipated, you must issue a **Legal Hold Letter**. This is a formal notice to your client to stop all automated deletion and data "cleaning." In Pt. 2, we’ll talk about how to actually *collect* that data without breaking the metadata.
