# Five Discovery Mistakes That Cost Texas Attorneys Real Money

In Texas litigation, discovery is where cases are won or lost—and where firms lose the most money to inefficiency and sanctions. 

Whether it's a "Level 1" expedited case or a complex multi-party commercial dispute, the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure (TRCP) are full of traps for the unwary. After 20 years of supporting Texas litigators, I’ve seen these five mistakes happen over and over again. They don’t just hurt your case; they hurt your bottom line.

### 1. Missing the "Automatic" Initial Disclosures
Since the 2021 amendments to TRCP 194, initial disclosures are now mandatory and automatic. You no longer wait for a request. If you’re late on these, you risk having your witnesses or documents excluded. Solos often get tripped up by the timing, especially in fast-moving cases.

### 2. The "Over-Inclusive" Document Dump
Sometimes, attorneys think that "giving them everything" is a good strategy to bury opposing counsel. In reality, you’re often just burying *yourself*. You end up paying for a paralegal or associate to review thousands of irrelevant documents, and you risk accidentally producing privileged information. Targeted, precise collection is always more profitable than a document dump.

### 3. The Failing Privilege Log
If you withhold a document, you *must* produce a privilege log that satisfies the requirements of TRCP 193.3. A vague, poorly drafted log is an invitation for a Motion to Compel and an expensive, time-consuming *in camera* review by the judge. Doing it right the first time saves thousands in needless briefing and hearing time.

### 4. Ignoring the ESI Protocol
Electronic Discovery (eDiscovery) is no longer "optional" for small firms. If you don't have a clear agreement on the format of production (metadata, searchable PDFs, native files), you will inevitably end up with a production that your software can't read—or worse, a production that opposing counsel rejects as "non-conforming."

### 5. Waiting Until the Last Minute to Summary
Deposition summaries are most valuable when they are done *immediately* after the deposition. Waiting six months until trial prep starts means you’ve lost the nuance and the opportunity to use those admissions for a Motion for Summary Judgment. It also leads to "rush fees" and overtime costs that eat into your contingency fee or your client's budget.
