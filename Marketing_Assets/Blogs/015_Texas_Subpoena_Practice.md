# Texas Subpoena Practice: What Solo Attorneys Often Get Wrong

In Texas litigation, the subpoena is one of your most powerful tools for gathering evidence from third parties. But it’s also one of the easiest to mess up. 

A single procedural error can lead to a Motion to Quash, a "Discovery Sanction," or—most commonly—a third party simply ignoring your request because the subpoena was "defective." Under **Texas Rules of Civil Procedure 176 and 205**, the requirements for a valid subpoena are technical and strict.

After coordinating hundreds of subpoenas across the state, I’ve noticed three recurring mistakes that solo attorneys make.

### 1. The "Notice to All Parties" Failure
Under TRCP 205.2, if you are serving a subpoena for production on a non-party, you *must* serve a notice on every other party in the case at least **10 days** before the subpoena is served. 
Many attorneys forget this 10-day "waiting period." If you serve the subpoena early, the other side can move to quash it, and the court will likely agree. 

### 2. Improper Geographic Reach
A Texas subpoena is powerful, but it has limits. Under TRCP 176.3, a person cannot be required to appear at a place more than **150 miles** from where they reside or are served. Solos often get tripped up by this when they try to subpoena a witness from Dallas to appear in a Houston court. 
**The Solution:** You have to take the deposition (or the document production) to them, or negotiate a Remote Online session.

### 3. The "Witness Fee" Oversight
A subpoena is not legally "served" in Texas unless it is accompanied by the statutory witness fee (currently **$10**). If your process server hands over the subpoena without the ten-dollar bill (or a check), the service is technically defective. For production-only subpoenas, you also need to be prepared to pay the "reasonable costs" of production if the third party requests it.

### 4. Vague "Descriptions of Documents"
A subpoena that asks for "any and all documents related to the case" is a "fishing expedition" and will be quashed. To be enforceable, the subpoena must describe the items to be produced with "reasonable particularity."
