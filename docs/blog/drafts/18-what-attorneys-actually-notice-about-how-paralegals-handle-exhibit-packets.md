# What Attorneys Actually Notice About How Paralegals Handle Exhibit Packets

*Pillar: Industry & Career | Target keyword: what attorneys notice paralegal exhibit packet quality*

---

Attorneys don't usually explain what they're evaluating when they hand you a document production task. They hand you the task, you do it, and their reaction tells you everything — either nothing (it was fine) or a lot (it wasn't).

Understanding what attorneys actually pay attention to in a finished exhibit packet helps you deliver work that earns trust — and trust is the currency that leads to better assignments, more autonomy, and a more secure professional position.

## The Things That Get Noticed First

**The order.** An attorney who hands you an organized exhibit list and gets back a packet in a different order notices immediately. Order problems surface fast because the attorney opens the packet expecting to find a specific document in a specific position. If it's not there, the search that follows is frustrating and memorable.

**The Bates numbering.** Attorneys who are experienced with document production scan Bates numbers automatically. A gap in the sequence, a duplicate, a numbering format that doesn't match prior productions — all of these stand out, because Bates numbers are used in briefs and depositions and opposing counsel will notice them too.

**The page count.** Experienced attorneys know approximately how many pages they expect in a production. A packet that's significantly shorter than expected prompts a question: what's missing?

**The cover documentation.** Does the production include a transmittal letter? Does the transmittal letter accurately describe what's included? Is it addressed correctly? These details are quick checks that reflect on the overall quality of the work.

## The Things That Get Noticed Over Time

**Consistency.** An attorney who works with the same paralegal over multiple productions develops expectations. If you always format Bates stamps with 5-digit zero-padding (`PL-EX-00001`), suddenly switching to 6 digits on the third production creates confusion and looks like a lack of attention to detail.

**Preparation.** When an attorney needs to find a document quickly — in a deposition, in a court conference — they rely on the Bates reference system to locate it. A paralegal who maintains clean, consistent Bates records across every production makes the attorney's job easier in ways that compound over months and years of working together.

**Problem-solving under pressure.** Every paralegal's most visible moments are deadline situations. How you handle an unexpected problem — a missing document, a numbering conflict, a production that needs to be corrected — is what attorneys remember. The ones who remain calm, communicate clearly, and deliver a resolution are the ones who earn trust. The ones who go silent, deliver a fix without explanation, or worse, deliver something that still has problems — those are the stories attorneys tell.

## The Things That Almost Never Get Noticed (But Should)

**The audit log.** Most attorneys don't look at the merge audit log — until they need it. When opposing counsel questions what was included in a production, or when a client asks why a specific document wasn't produced, the audit log is the first thing the attorney will ask for. If you're producing one automatically, you're already ahead of this problem.

**The security of the workflow.** Attorneys generally don't ask how you're processing documents. They assume you're doing it correctly. But if a data incident occurs involving client documents that were processed through a cloud tool without authorization — that assumption stops protecting you.

The attorneys who are informed about workflow security — who know their paralegal uses local-only document processing — are the ones whose clients are actually protected. The ones who never asked are the ones who might be unpleasantly surprised.

## What Builds a Reputation

A reputation as a reliable document production paralegal is built on:

1. Accurate ordering, every time
2. Correct and consistent Bates numbering
3. Proactive communication about the status of work
4. Clean, complete documentation (audit logs, transmittal letters)
5. Calm professionalism when problems arise

None of these require extraordinary skill. They require consistent application of good process. The paralegal who delivers this, reliably, is the one who gets the most important matters — because they've demonstrated they can be trusted with them.
