# Does Your PDF Software Comply With Your Firm's Confidentiality Policy?

*Pillar: Security & Compliance | Target keyword: law firm confidentiality policy PDF software compliance*

---

Most paralegals can tell you what their firm's confidentiality policy says in broad strokes. Don't share client information. Don't discuss cases outside the office. Keep files secure.

What most paralegals can't tell you is whether the PDF software they used this morning complies with that policy.

This is a gap worth closing — not because you're doing anything wrong, but because "I didn't know" is not a defense when a client asks why their documents were processed on a third-party server.

## The Policy Was Written Before the Tool Existed

Most law firm confidentiality policies were written years ago, updated incrementally, and weren't designed with cloud-based document tools in mind. They cover the obvious cases: don't email client files to the wrong person, don't leave papers on the copier, use encrypted drives for sensitive data.

What they often don't say — because they couldn't anticipate it — is whether uploading a client's merger agreement to a free online PDF compressor is acceptable.

That ambiguity is where most violations happen. Not from malice, but from policies that didn't keep up with the tools that became available.

## The Questions Your Policy Should Answer (But Probably Doesn't)

When evaluating a document tool against your firm's confidentiality obligations, the relevant questions are:

**Does the tool upload files to a third-party server?**
If yes, that server is now holding client-privileged information. Does your firm's policy authorize that? Does the attorney who supervises your work know it's happening?

**What is the tool's data retention policy?**
How long does the service keep a copy of processed files? If there's a breach two weeks from now, are your client's documents still in that system?

**Is the tool covered by a Business Associate Agreement or similar contract?**
Cloud services that handle client data often need to be formally vetted and contracted. Has your firm's IT department or managing partner signed off on this tool?

**Where are the servers located?**
Some client matters involve parties or information subject to international data protection regulations. Processing documents on servers in a different country may create compliance issues you didn't anticipate.

**Has anyone read the terms of service?**
This sounds obvious, but the answer is almost always no. The data usage section is where you'll find out what the tool actually does with your files.

## How to Audit the Tools You're Currently Using

You don't need to be technical to do a basic audit. For each tool you use to process client documents:

1. Open the terms of service and search for "data," "files," "retention," and "third party"
2. Note what the tool says about how long it keeps uploaded content
3. Check whether there is a privacy policy that addresses business/professional use separately from personal use
4. Ask your supervising attorney or IT contact whether the tool has been approved

If the tool is browser-based or cloud-hosted and hasn't been specifically approved, the safest default is: don't use it for client documents.

## The Cleanest Solution

The cleanest way to resolve the compliance question for document production tools is to use software that operates entirely on your machine. Local software doesn't upload files, doesn't retain them on external servers, and doesn't require a terms-of-service review for data handling — because there is no data handling beyond your own hard drive.

This isn't the only acceptable solution. Vetted cloud tools with proper agreements can also be compliant. But local software makes the compliance question simple: the files never go anywhere, so the policy answer is always yes.
