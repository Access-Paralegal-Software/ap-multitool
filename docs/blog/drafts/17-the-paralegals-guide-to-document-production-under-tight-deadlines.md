# The Paralegal's Guide to Document Production Under Tight Deadlines

*Pillar: Industry & Career | Target keyword: paralegal document production deadline pressure guide*

---

Deadline pressure in litigation is not occasional. It's structural. Discovery cutoffs, filing deadlines, court-ordered production timelines — they stack up against each other in ways that regularly produce situations where you have more documents to process than time to process them in.

This guide is about the mental model and practical tactics that experienced paralegals use to execute production under pressure without making the kinds of errors that create secondary problems.

## Deadline Pressure Is Not a Reason to Skip Verification

The most common mistake paralegals make under deadline pressure is skipping verification steps because "there's no time." The logic is understandable: there are 200 documents to process in two hours, and the review step feels like a luxury.

It isn't. The verification step is what separates a production that goes out clean from one that needs to be corrected — which takes more time, more explanation, and more stress than the original review would have required.

Under deadline pressure, keep every verification step. What you should eliminate is everything that isn't verification:

- Eliminate the time you spend renaming files for sort order (use a queue instead)
- Eliminate the time you spend manually tracking Bates numbers (use a registry)
- Eliminate the time you spend in back-and-forth email asking for the case prefix (have a standard format)
- Eliminate the time you spend re-assembling because the first attempt had an ordering error (review the queue before you merge)

The goal is to protect your verification time by eliminating every other inefficiency in the workflow.

## Triage Before You Start

When a large production lands on your desk with a tight deadline, the first 10 minutes should be spent triaging — not producing. Ask:

**What is the actual deadline?** Is it a court filing deadline (immovable), an opposing counsel due date (sometimes negotiable with a call), or an attorney's internal target (usually has some flexibility)?

**What is the complete document set?** Don't start processing until you have a definitive inventory. Starting before the set is complete means potentially reprocessing when additional documents surface.

**Are any documents unavailable?** Missing documents discovered at the beginning of the production window are manageable. Discovered at the end, they're a crisis.

**What Bates prefix and start number applies?** Five minutes spent confirming this prevents a full reprinting if you start at the wrong number.

Ten minutes of triage prevents three hours of recovery.

## Processing Large Volumes Efficiently

**Work in batches if the volume is very large.** For productions of 200+ documents, consider processing in logical batches — all medical records, then all correspondence, then all financial documents — and merging the batches into a final combined packet. This lets you verify each segment independently and makes it easier to identify a problem if one occurs.

**Use background processing.** If your production tool supports background threading — processing documents without freezing the interface — take advantage of it. While a batch is processing, you can be preparing the next batch, reviewing the audit log from the previous one, or handling the attorney communications that always come up during production.

**Don't wait for perfect before you start.** If you have 90% of the documents and you're waiting on two records, start the queue setup and ordering for what you have. When the remaining documents arrive, you'll be able to add them to the queue and confirm their position in minutes.

## Communication During Production

Keep the supervising attorney informed, concisely and proactively:

- "I have all documents loaded, ordering now, on track for the 3 PM deadline"
- "Processing is running, should be complete by 2:30"
- "Production is complete and verified, attaching now"

This eliminates the check-in interruptions that break your concentration during production. A brief status update every 30–45 minutes is almost always enough.

If you hit a problem — a document that won't open, a Bates number conflict, a file that's missing — communicate it immediately and with a proposed resolution. "We're missing Exhibit 14, the Johnson contract. I can get a copy from the Dropbox share in 10 minutes and still make the deadline" is a complete problem statement with a solution. That's what you want to deliver.

## After the Deadline

Once the production is out, complete one final step: archive the audit log and update your production index for the case. Do this immediately, while the context is fresh.

Future you — the you who is asked in six months what was included in the March production — will be grateful.
