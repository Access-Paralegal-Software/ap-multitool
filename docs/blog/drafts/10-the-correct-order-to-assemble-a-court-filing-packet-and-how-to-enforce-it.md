# The Correct Order to Assemble a Court Filing Packet (And How to Enforce It)

*Pillar: Workflow & How-To | Target keyword: court filing packet document order assembly paralegal*

---

Ask five paralegals how to order a court filing packet and you'll get five slightly different answers. The truth is, exhibit ordering conventions vary by jurisdiction, court, judge, and case type. What doesn't vary is the consequence of getting it wrong: a rejection, a callback from the clerk's office, or an attorney standing at a filing deadline asking why the exhibits are out of order.

This post is about the principles behind exhibit packet ordering, how to determine the right order for your specific filing, and how to enforce that order without errors when you're assembling a large packet.

## The Three Layers of Ordering Rules

Exhibit packet order is governed by three layers of rules, each narrowing the one above it:

**1. Jurisdiction rules.** Federal district courts and state courts often have local rules about how exhibits should be organized in submissions — chronologically, by document type, or by party. These are published in local rules documents and are the baseline standard.

**2. Judge-specific practices.** Many judges have chambers practices or standing orders that go beyond local rules. These may specify how exhibits should be tabbed, what kind of separator sheets are required, or whether Bates stamps should appear on a specific part of the page. Check the judge's standing orders, available on most court websites.

**3. Attorney preference.** After jurisdictional and judicial requirements are met, the supervising attorney may have a preferred organization style for exhibits in a particular case. Some attorneys organize chronologically. Others organize by witness or by issue. Clarify this before you start assembling.

## Common Ordering Frameworks

**Chronological:** Documents arranged by date, oldest to newest. Most common in cases where timeline is the central issue — employment disputes, personal injury, breach of contract.

**By document type:** All medical records together, all correspondence together, all financial records together. Common in complex litigation with many exhibit types.

**By witness:** Documents grouped by the witness they support or come from. Common in deposition support packets.

**By issue:** Documents grouped by the legal issue they address. Common in motion practice where exhibits need to map to specific arguments.

The choice between these frameworks depends on the case and the attorney's strategy. The important thing is to confirm the framework before you start, not after.

## Why Exhibit Ordering Is a Production Problem

The difficulty isn't knowing the right order — it's enforcing that order accurately when you're assembling a large packet with many individual documents.

When you're manually reordering 40 PDFs across a file system, errors happen. A file gets put in the wrong subfolder. A rename-and-sort sequence gets disrupted. You assemble what you think is the right order and the attorney finds a misplaced document at 4 PM the day before the filing deadline.

The reliable solution is a visual queue where you can see the exact order before committing to a merge.

## How to Enforce Your Order Without Errors

A document queue that reflects the actual merge order before processing is the single most effective tool for preventing sequence errors. The workflow looks like this:

1. Load all source documents into the queue
2. Arrange them in the correct order using drag-and-drop or explicit position entry
3. Review the queue visually — top to bottom, matching against your outline or exhibit list
4. If any document needs to move to a specific position, enter that position number directly rather than dragging through a long list
5. Merge only after confirming the queue matches your intended order

The key principle: **the queue is the source of truth, not the file system.** Don't try to enforce order through file naming or folder organization — enforce it in the queue where you can see it.

## The Override Move for Large Queues

When you have 50 documents in a queue and need to bump one specific document to position 3, dragging it from position 47 through 44 intermediate positions is slow and error-prone.

The faster method: double-click the position number (`#` column) on the document you want to move, type the target position number, and press Enter. The document jumps to that position and everything else adjusts automatically.

This is especially useful for cover sheets, certification pages, or priority exhibits that need to precede the main body of a packet.
